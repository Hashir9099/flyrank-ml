Search Intelligence · CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring

Do top-ranking pages always earn the clicks their position should predict?

Using 198,480 published content items from FlyRank's pseudonymized search-performance warehouse, I built an expected-click-through-rate benchmark by search position, then scored every page against it to find pages that under-capture clicks relative to peers at the same rank. The resulting opportunity list is impression-weighted, carries a reason code per page, and surfaces a real anomaly — position 11-20 out-clicks position 6-10 — that a smoothed curve would have hidden. This is decision-support for a content team choosing what to review next, not a claim about why any single page underperforms.

Introduction

The decision this supports

A content team with hundreds of published pages can't manually audit every one for "is this doing as well as it should." They need a ranked, explainable shortlist.

Position alone is a poor proxy for success — a page can rank well and still under-perform its peers at that same rank, silently losing clicks to a weak title, a mismatched snippet, or a SERP feature eating the top spot. This work builds an empirical benchmark for "what CTR should a page at this position expect," then flags pages that fall short of it by a meaningful, traffic-weighted margin — turning a vague "something might be wrong" into a ranked list a human can act on.

Methodology

Building the benchmark, then scoring against it

Step 1 — the benchmark. CTR is heavily zero-inflated at the individual-page level (60.8% of pages had zero clicks in the window), so a single page's own CTR is too noisy to trust alone. The benchmark instead sums clicks and impressions within each position bucket before dividing — an impression-weighted rate, not an average of noisy per-page rates.

Step 2 — bucket design. An initial 7-bucket split showed a thin tail (322 pages at "100+") reversing the expected decay — clear small-sample noise. Buckets were merged to 6 (51+) to remove that artifact, while the separate 11-20 vs. 6-10 anomaly was kept, since both of those buckets are large enough (41k / 62k pages) that merging them would hide a real pattern rather than fix noise.

Step 3 — scoring. Each page's ctr_gap is its bucket's expected CTR minus its actual CTR; opportunity_score weights that gap by the page's own impression volume, so high-traffic underperformers rank above low-traffic ones with an identical percentage gap. Every scored page carries a reason code:

underperforming_for_position

Real CTR gap, meaningful impression volume (≥100) — the core opportunity list.

gap_but_low_volume

A gap exists, but on too little traffic (<100 impressions) to act on with confidence.

meeting_or_beating_expected_ctr

At or above its position's benchmark — no action indicated.

Limitations & honest framing

What this cannot claim

  • Known weighting issue, not yet resolved: the current opportunity_score is dominated by a small number of very-high-impression pages from a handful of clients. A future revision should normalize per-client or log-transform impressions so the list reflects genuine relative underperformance, not just raw scale.
  • The 6-10 vs. 11-20 anomaly is unexplained, not resolved. Plausible causes — SERP feature crowding at 6-10, or an intent-mix difference between buckets — are named but not tested against this data.
  • One month of data (the panel's final month sample). This is a snapshot, not a trend — it says nothing about whether a page's gap is new or long-standing.
  • Correlational only. A CTR gap flags a page for human review; it does not diagnose the cause (title, meta description, SERP feature, intent mismatch) or prove that fixing anything would change the outcome.
  • No causal claim about search-engine ranking behavior is made or implied anywhere in this work.
Ranked recommendations

What to do with this list

  1. Review underperforming_for_position pages first, sorted by opportunity_score — largest weighted gap, highest confidence in the signal.
  2. Treat gap_but_low_volume as a watchlist, not an action list — re-check after the page accumulates more impressions.
  3. Fix the impression-weighting before using this for client-facing prioritization — see Limitations; as-is, the list over-indexes on a few large clients.
  4. Investigate the 11-20/6-10 anomaly with a SERP-feature audit before assuming it's noise, since sample size rules that out.
